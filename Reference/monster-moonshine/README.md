# Monster and moonshine: a sourced starting point

Read Borcherds’s [What Is the Monster?](https://math.berkeley.edu/~reb/papers/whatismonster/whatismonster.pdf) first. The seven canonical references include accessible explanations, original conjectures, a primary proof, a historical survey, a historical problem list and ATLAS. They are reading links because a general permission to republish the originals was not established. [Catalogue](../CATALOGUE.md#monster-moonshine).

The Monster is the largest **sporadic finite simple group**. It is not the largest finite group: cyclic groups exist for arbitrarily large finite orders. This framing and the Monster’s relation to moonshine are introduced in [Borcherds, What Is Moonshine?, opening page](https://math.berkeley.edu/~reb/papers/icm98/icm98.pdf). The cyclic-group counterargument is an elementary deduction, not a claim that there is a largest member among all finite groups.

[ATLAS, opening order statement](https://brauer.maths.qmul.ac.uk/Atlas/spor/M/) records

\[
|\mathbb M|=808017424794512875886459904961710757005754368000000000.
\]

Use that stable fact separately from the page’s old computing-resource updates and historical maximal-subgroup completeness statements.

An introductory glimpse of moonshine is the numerical relation `196884 = 1 + 196883`, connecting a coefficient in the classical modular `j` expansion to dimensions of Monster representations. A numerical coincidence by itself proves no correspondence; the deeper theory involves graded representations, modular functions and more structure. [Borcherds, What Is Moonshine?, first page](https://math.berkeley.edu/~reb/papers/icm98/icm98.pdf).

[Conway–Norton 1979](https://londmathsoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1112/blms/11.3.308) is the original moonshine reference. [Borcherds 1992, Theorem 1.1 and §9](https://math.berkeley.edu/~reb/papers/monster/monster.pdf) is a primary proof reference. [Gannon’s survey](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0402345v2) is useful historical guidance, not a current inventory of open problems.

## Prerequisite route

1. Sets, functions, equivalence relations and proof reasoning.
2. Finite groups, actions, normal subgroups and quotients.
3. Linear algebra, representations and characters.
4. Complex analysis and modular forms/functions.
5. For the proof: graded spaces, vertex algebras and generalized Kac–Moody constructions.

The final items are later branches requiring additional sources and study. This is an editorial learning route, not a theorem deriving Monster symmetry from shape grammars. A planar design being complicated does not establish an action of the Monster. A proposed connection must specify the objects, transformations, operation and evidence.
