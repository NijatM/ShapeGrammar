## What it is about

Bier and Knight introduce an issue of Footprint devoted to architecture that uses digital systems to sense, respond and change. Their subject extends from digitally designing and fabricating a building to operating the built structure through feedback from its users and surroundings.

[pp. 1–2: definition and historical framing](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## The question

What happens when architecture can change during use? The editorial asks how kinetic, responsive and interactive structures might address changing spatial needs, rather than treating the completed building as a permanently fixed configuration.

[pp. 1–2: definition and historical framing](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## Central premise

The authors frame a shift from standardized, static production toward customizable and adaptable environments. They propose that digital control can support both practical flexibility and sensory or emotional relationships with buildings; these are possibilities to investigate, not measured benefits established by the editorial.

[pp. 1–2: definition and historical framing](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892) · [pp. 2–4: five contributions and concluding synthesis](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## How the argument works

A short historical account connects contemporary systems with cybernetics, Archigram and kinetic architecture. The editors then compare five contributions spanning interactive design history, transformable structures, cultural interpretations, responsive installations and audiovisual extensions between spaces.

[pp. 1–2: definition and historical framing](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892) · [pp. 2–4: five contributions and concluding synthesis](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## Examples to watch

The issue includes a scissor-pair transformable structure, an InteractiveWall combining sound, light and movement, and mediated windows connecting remote locations. These examples broaden interaction beyond mechanical movement to perception and communication. The descriptions belong to this editorial; detailed technical evidence is in the individual contributions.

[pp. 2–4: five contributions and concluding synthesis](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## Conclusion and limits

The editors argue that feedback-driven structures open new design possibilities, while identifying obstacles such as building regulations written around static forms. They provide no comparative energy, cost, safety or occupancy trial that establishes responsive architecture as generally superior.

[pp. 2–4: five contributions and concluding synthesis](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)

## What to take into an experiment

Distinguish designing an object digitally from designing its ongoing behaviour. For a responsive prototype, specify what it senses, what it changes, whose needs it serves and how feedback affects the next action. Use the cited projects to investigate those choices further.

[pp. 1–2: definition and historical framing](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892) · [pp. 2–4: five contributions and concluding synthesis](https://journals.open.tudelft.nl/footprint/article/download/715/892)
