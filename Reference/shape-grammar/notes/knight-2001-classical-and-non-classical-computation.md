## What it is about

Knight and Stiny separate two questions often collapsed in discussions of computational design: how things are represented and how a process is understood or evaluated. Their classical/non-classical taxonomy is not a distinction between using and avoiding computers.

[pp. 355–358: representation, process and four-way taxonomy; Figs. 1–5](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf)

## Central premise

A system can pursue surprising outcomes while still representing everything with fixed primitives. Conversely, it can use visually reinterpretable shapes within an explicit, understandable rule process. The authors argue that these combinations matter for supporting design.

[pp. 355–358: representation, process and four-way taxonomy; Figs. 1–5](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf)

## The four categories

Classical representation uses defined primitives and fixed combinations; non-classical representation leaves shapes open to reinterpretation. Classical process values explicit explanatory rules; non-classical process emphasizes results without requiring a comparable explanation. Crossing the two dimensions yields four categories, with boundaries the authors acknowledge are not always hard.

[pp. 355–358: representation, process and four-way taxonomy; Figs. 1–5](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf)

## How the argument is made

The selected passages compare language-like representations, spatial examples and shape-grammar computations. A square-and-diamond drawing can be seen as different collections of figures, while the text distinguishes a rule system’s descriptive explanation from the usefulness of its results.

[pp. 355–358: representation, process and four-way taxonomy; Figs. 1–5](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf) · [pp. 364, 367–370: emergence and ambiguity; Figs. 19–22](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf)

## An example worth following

Emergent hexagons or fish can become targets for later rules. The distinction among anticipated, possible and unanticipated emergence depends on what the grammar author or user expected and prepared for. Ambiguity allows a current figure to be seen through different parts rather than preserving only its original assembly.

[pp. 364, 367–370: emergence and ambiguity; Figs. 19–22](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight%26Stiny.classicalAndNonClassicalComputation.pdf)

## What the selected argument supports

Shape computation provides a formal way to act on newly perceived parts. Explaining a design with one fixed decomposition need not exhaust what can be seen in it. This is a conceptual account supported by worked diagrams, not a controlled comparison of creative performance.

## A useful two-question check

When evaluating a design tool, ask what its representation fixes in advance, then ask what explanation its process makes available. Those questions are independent. A visually displayed model may still have fixed symbolic parts underneath.
