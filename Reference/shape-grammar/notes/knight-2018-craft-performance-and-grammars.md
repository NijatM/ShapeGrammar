## Short overview

Knight compares three grammars for kolam: a traditional pattern-making practice in Tamil Nadu. Two shape grammars generate finished designs using modular motifs or mirror curves. A making grammar instead follows a growing line through paired acts of seeing and drawing. The comparison shows how a similar pattern can arise through very different constructive strategies.

[pp.205–210: §§1–3, craft performance and temporal segmentation](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=1) · [pp.210–215: §4, modular motifs and mirror curves](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=6) · [pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12)

## Question and premise

What is lost when computational craft descriptions focus on the object produced? The chapter argues that a craft’s temporal, embodied performance deserves attention alongside the final design. Making rules can help describe selected parts of that performance, even though no finite description captures everything a maker does or perceives.

[pp.205–210: §§1–3, craft performance and temporal segmentation](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=1)

## The proposed distinction

Shape rules segment the spatial content of a design. Making rules can additionally mark changes of sensory attention and temporal breakpoints in an activity. Their segmentation is a chosen interpretation: the chapter makes no claim to a universal account of how people perceive events, and leaves the performer and physical setting outside its making-grammar representation.

[pp.205–210: §§1–3, craft performance and temporal segmentation](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=1)

## Method and comparison

The study is an illustrative theoretical comparison informed by recorded performances and published first-hand accounts. The modular grammar places motifs symmetrically in grid cells. The mirror-curve grammar fixes mirrors, then traces a line through the grid. The proposed line-on-a-walk grammar uses pulli dots and paired seeing/drawing rules for straight continuations and different turns, with gesture complexity guiding how much of the path a step covers.

[pp.210–215: §4, modular motifs and mirror curves](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=6) · [pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12)

## What the examples show

The first two grammars are stated to generate the same design possibilities, and the chapter works through the same example pattern by each method. Yet neither captures live performance well: modular placement differs from weaving a line, while mirrors set in advance suggest a fixed plan. The making grammar exposes a different structure—looking ahead, extending a line and choosing turns during making—illustrated in Figs.10–11.

[pp.210–215: §4, modular motifs and mirror curves](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=6) · [pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12)

## Outcome and conclusion

The contribution is a comparison of explanatory aims and generative strategies, rather than proof that one grammar is universally better. A longer making grammar can explain aspects of performance that a concise pattern generator leaves out. Knight proposes that alternative temporal segmentations may suggest new making strategies, while explicitly noting that changing kolam’s segmentations might leave its pattern possibilities unchanged.

[pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12) · [pp.223–224: discussion and references](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=19)

## What remains incomplete

The line-on-a-walk rules do not encode all symmetry planning or the addition of pulli during making. They are more open-ended than the two shape grammars, so the chapter does not establish that all three generate exactly the same language. Gesture boundaries are conjectural simplifications; duration is not explored. Socio-cultural inquiry is a possible later use, not an empirical result demonstrated here.

[pp.205–210: §§1–3, craft performance and temporal segmentation](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=1) · [pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12) · [pp.223–224: discussion and references](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=19)

## What to notice while experimenting

Compare the final geometry with the sequence used to make it. Identify where a rule changes the drawing, where it changes attention, and why a step ends where it does. A visual identity-rule experiment can clarify the attention/geometry distinction, but it does not reproduce kolam performance, gesture timing or the curved rules shown in this chapter.

[pp.205–210: §§1–3, craft performance and temporal segmentation](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=1) · [pp.216–222: §4.3 and Figs.10–11, line on a walk](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=12) · [pp.223–224: discussion and references](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-10-8189-7_16.pdf#page=19)

## Useful terms

- **Pulli:** A dot in the organizational arrangement used in the kolam examples.
- **Mirror curve:** A path constructed by reflecting an imagined ray within a grid bounded and divided by mirrors.
- **Temporal segmentation:** A chosen division of a continuous making activity into actions or moments.
