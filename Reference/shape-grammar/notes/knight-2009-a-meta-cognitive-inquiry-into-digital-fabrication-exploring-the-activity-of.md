# A Meta-Cognitive Inquiry into Digital Fabrication: Exploring the Activity of Designing and Making of a Wall Screen

## What it is about

Arpak, Sass and Knight follow the design and making of a patterned wall screen to explore the interaction of visual reasoning, material behaviour and assembly. The intended object is a self-supporting timber system, while the reported final prototypes are small laser-cut cardboard assemblies.

[printed pp. 475–476: research question and experiment](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf) · [printed pp. 477–480: iterative phases and prototypes; Figs. 1–10](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## Research question

Can a designer observe and reconsider her own decisions while moving between drawing, modelling and fabrication? The concern is metacognition: stepping back from an action to reinterpret the process and learn from it.

[printed pp. 475–476: research question and experiment](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## How the study was carried out

Arpak reflects on her own project. The report retrospectively organizes overlapping activity into pattern selection and decomposition, structural explorations, material/joint refinement, and final prototyping/assembly. Rapid prototypes and laser-cut models provide feedback that revises visual decisions.

[printed pp. 475–476: research question and experiment](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf) · [printed pp. 477–480: iterative phases and prototypes; Figs. 1–10](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## Concrete observations

Three Islamic architectural patterns provide initial visual material. Structural interlocking introduces new decompositions and layering; a three-layer arrangement separates a tessellated structural layer from external units. Four-inch and twelve-inch cardboard prototypes reveal opportunities for varied edges, openings, customized pieces and assembly sequences.

[printed pp. 477–480: iterative phases and prototypes; Figs. 1–10](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## What the authors conclude

Visual analysis helps produce components and assemblies, while structural constraints generate unexpected visual alternatives. Material choices, structure, assembly order and production techniques create productive conflicts throughout the project; making is part of design rather than only its final execution.

[printed pp. 477–480: iterative phases and prototypes; Figs. 1–10](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf) · [printed pp. 480–482: conclusion, limitations and references](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## What remains unproven

The self-reflective observations may be biased and are not a general cognitive experiment. The article’s suggestion of faster learning is not backed by a comparative learning-time measure. Small prototypes do not establish full-scale timber performance, cost, durability or structural compliance.

[printed pp. 477–480: iterative phases and prototypes; Figs. 1–10](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf) · [printed pp. 480–482: conclusion, limitations and references](https://ecaade.org/current/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/eCAADe_2009.pdf)

## How to try the method

Keep a decision log while making a small prototype. Record the visual intention, the physical conflict encountered, the revision made and what prompted it. Distinguish a new visual possibility from a verified construction solution, and test at the relevant scale before making a stronger claim.
