## Short overview

Knight studies emergence in shape grammars: a computation can produce shapes not explicitly predefined, and these can then become inputs for further computation.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)

## Question and premise

How can a grammar act on something its author did not anticipate? The abstract distinguishes anticipated, possible and unanticipated emergence in relation to the grammar author or user.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)

## Method reported

The paper combines historical discussion with a theoretical classification and proposed special rules. It describes examples both within one grammar and across parallel grammars.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)

## Reported contribution

For some problems, an action can be specified in advance even when its eventual shape target is not. The proposed rules bring certain operations previously performed outside a grammar into the grammar itself.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)

## What remains unchecked

The abstract does not establish that every unanticipated shape can be handled by a predefined rule. The definitions and detailed examples need full-text review.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)

## Why it matters here

Distinguish seeing a new shape from having a rule that can act on it. Keep track of which possibilities your grammar anticipates and which require new choices.

[Public abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1068/b12914)
