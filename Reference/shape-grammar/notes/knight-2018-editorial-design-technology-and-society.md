## Short overview

Cardoso Llach and Knight introduce a six-paper track examining technology in the design of the built environment. Their framing brings historical, social and material questions to computation and fabrication: how technologies shape creativity, labor, material practice and the production of data.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Question and premise

How can design research examine technology beyond treating it as a neutral tool or a promise of smoother production? The editorial asks readers to consider the institutions, choices, skills and histories through which technological systems become part of design practice.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Editorial position

The track treats technology and data as situated in social and material circumstances. The editors emphasize that conceptions of creativity, materiality and labor change alongside technologies. This is an interpretive framing of the contributions, rather than a new experimental claim tested by the editorial itself.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## How the editorial develops its argument

The editors organize six contributions into three pairs: laboratory histories in design and computing; the construction and political implications of data; and creative practice studied through design fiction and ethnography. Their method here is a short thematic introduction and comparison. The archival studies, cases and observations belong to the individual papers they describe.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Examples introduced

One pair examines Olivetti’s computing laboratory and five design-education laboratory models. Another examines data creation in an Alaska research project and two digital cycling-quantification regimes in Santiago. The final pair considers speculative writing interfaces and the frictions encountered when a design-build group moves among digital media, drawings, physical models and construction.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Conclusion and reading value

The editors conclude that the papers offer ways to rethink technology through historical, ethnographic and design-fictional inquiry. A useful thread is that transitions among representations, institutions and materials involve decisions and resistance. The editorial reports no pooled dataset, measured improvement or single causal result for the track.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Questions to take into the project

When a digital model feels exact or seamless, ask which decisions, skills and material behavior it leaves outside the representation. Follow the relevant individual paper for evidence about a particular setting; use this editorial as a map of questions, not as independent confirmation of every introduced study.

[Printed pp.3–4 (deposited volume PDF pp.28–29): complete editorial](https://www.aacademica.org/del.giorgio.solfa/228.pdf#page=28)

## Useful terms

- **Seamful data:** The introduced data paper’s approach to making choices and discontinuities in data work visible.
- **Design fiction:** A speculative design method used by one introduced contribution to explore possible writing interfaces.
