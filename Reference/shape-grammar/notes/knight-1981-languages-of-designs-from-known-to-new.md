## In brief

Knight shifts attention from generating designs within a style to generating new design languages by changing the spatial relations that support them.

## Question

Can existing compositional knowledge become a systematic starting point for invention?

Evidence: [pp. 213–217: Languages of designs; defining new languages](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## Premise and claim

Replacing constituent shapes inside known spatial relations yields new relations from which new grammars can be constructed.

Evidence: [pp. 213–217: Languages of designs; defining new languages](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## How it works

Define a two-way shape-equivalence rule with a specified geometric alignment. Apply it repeatedly to shapes in a spatial relation; parameterized schemata handle families of shapes.

Evidence: [pp. 217–225: Shape replacement, equivalence rules and schemata](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## Examples

Square–triangle and rectangle–trapezoid examples produce alternative relation families. Round-to-pointed arch replacement gives a deliberately elementary Romanesque–Gothic comparison.

Evidence: [pp. 217–225: Shape replacement, equivalence rules and schemata](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf); [pp. 225–238: examples, classes and concluding remarks](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## What follows

Repeated replacements can define finite or infinite classes of spatial relations. These supply multiple possible languages, rather than merely variants of one finished drawing.

Evidence: [pp. 225–238: examples, classes and concluding remarks](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## Limits

A design or corpus permits several grammatical interpretations. The paper proposes descriptive possibilities; it does not establish that historical designers consciously used these replacement rules.

Evidence: [pp. 213–217: Languages of designs; defining new languages](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf); [pp. 225–238: examples, classes and concluding remarks](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).

## Why read it

Distinguish changing a design with a rule from changing the relations used to design an entire grammar.

Evidence: [pp. 213–217: Languages of designs; defining new languages](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf); [pp. 217–225: Shape replacement, equivalence rules and schemata](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/48-747/subFrames/readings/Knight.languagesOfDesignsFromKnownToNew.pdf).
