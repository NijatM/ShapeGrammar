## What it is about

Knight traces how shape-grammar research has represented physical things and material properties. The paper then proposes reversing the usual starting point: begin with what materials do and how makers sense them, rather than making every material object behave first like a visual shape.

[Introduction and Shapes/Shapes and Other Descriptions, pp. 963–971](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## Premise and claim

Visual shape operations are useful for creative perception, but may be misleading for physical objects. Knight argues that a computational theory of making should preserve the relevant behaviour of its materials while retaining shape grammar’s open-ended relationship between perception and action.

[Complications, pp. 972–974; Figs. 3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## Method of argument

A historical review distinguishes set-based objects, parallel descriptions and integrated properties such as colours or weights. Counterexamples reveal problems with using shape operations indiscriminately. The paper then summarizes Knight and Stiny’s making-grammar framework through doing, sensing, stuff and things.

[Introduction and Shapes/Shapes and Other Descriptions, pp. 963–971](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Complications, pp. 972–974; Figs. 3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## Examples that carry the argument

Subtracting the line representation of one adjacent cube also removes a shared face-edge structure from the other; conversely, adjoining solids can merge and lose their separate identities. Knotting supplies a contrasting model: strings stay distinct when combined, and sensing rules move hand grasps without necessarily changing the string’s configuration.

[Complications, pp. 972–974; Figs. 3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## Proposed conclusion

Shapes become one kind of thing that a grammar can make, rather than the mandatory foundation for every material description. Designing with shapes can itself be understood as making: seeing and drawing are specialized sensory and active engagements.

[Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## Limits and unfinished directions

The knotting grammar is deliberately schematic. Watercolour painting is described as a more complex prospective case, not a completed formalization. Choosing an algebra for a material is not the same as predicting all its physical properties or demonstrating a working fabrication system.

[Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)

## What this changes for a model

Ask whether elements should fuse, stay discrete, deform, or acquire irreversible changes. Identify the doing and sensing actions your rules express. A useful representation depends on the activity being studied; visually identical geometry may correspond to materially different things.

[Complications, pp. 972–974; Figs. 3–4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3) · [Things and Discussion, pp. 974–978; Figs. 5–7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00004-015-0267-3)
